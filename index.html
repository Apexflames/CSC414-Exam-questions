★★★ Very likely📄 Past paper1a · 5 marks

Define Human-Computer Interaction and explain its importance in software development.

Definition. Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) is the study, planning and design of how people interact with computers. It is basically the interaction between people (end-users) and computers. It is interdisciplinary, drawing on computer science, psychology, design, cognitive science, ergonomics, linguistics and artificial intelligence.

Importance in software development:

  • Usability and user experience. Improving usability and UX is the primary goal of HCI. A system can be technically correct and still be hard to use (the CampusChef example: a great idea, a terrible experience, deleted after five minutes).
  • Efficiency and fewer errors. A clear layout helps users finish tasks faster with fewer mistakes.
  • User acceptance and retention. Poorly designed interfaces drive users away permanently, especially when alternatives exist.
  • Lower cost. Less training, fewer support calls, and less rework because usability problems are found early.
  • Trust. A well-structured interface makes a system look reliable. A poor one makes a powerful system look unreliable.
  • Lower stress. A clean, intuitive interface reduces cognitive load and frustration.

1. The system (a Voice User Interface). A VUI lets users interact through spoken commands. It is hands-free, natural, and supports users with certain disabilities, so it fits users who cannot read a screen.

2. How it works (illustrative dialogue, design suggestion):

System : Welcome. Say "balance", "transfer" or "help". User : Balance. System : You asked for your account balance. Please say your PIN. User : (speaks / enters PIN) System : Your balance is fifty-two thousand naira. Say "repeat" to hear it again, or "menu" to go back.

3. HCI principles applied:

PrincipleHow it applies here
Feedback (Shneiderman 3)The system confirms what it heard ("You asked for your balance") and speaks a result. Sound is the main feedback channel because sight is not available.
Design dialogs to yield closure (4)End each task clearly: "That is your balance. Anything else?"
Prevent errors (5) and handle errors (Human errors)Ask for confirmation of risky or unclear commands. When speech is not understood, explain and invite a retry: "Sorry, I did not catch that. Please say balance, transfer or help." Never use a code such as "Error 4738".
Reversal and control (6, 7)Commands such as "repeat", "back", "cancel" and "stop" are always available, so the user is in charge.
Reduce memory load (8) · Cognitive loadOffer only 3 or 4 short options at a time and read them out. Do not make users remember long menus.
Consistency (1)Same wording, same voice and same command names throughout.
Clarity and simple languageShort sentences in everyday words. Numbers spoken clearly.
Multimodal optionAdd touch or haptic or keypad input as a backup (voice + touch), because voice alone has problems in noise.
Accessibility and ethicsDesigned with visually impaired users, not for them. Respect privacy (see below).

4. Challenges to mention (from the notes): accent differences, background noise, speech recognition errors, privacy concerns (a balance read aloud in public), and difficulty presenting complex visual information by voice alone.

5. Suggested safeguards: a confirmation step before reading sensitive information, a PIN entered by keypad or touch instead of spoken aloud, and an option to use earphones.

What are the key components of HCI?

Note: the notes have no single numbered list titled "key components". This answer is assembled from what the notes do define, which is how you should present it.

  1. The user (human). The person with senses (sight, touch, hearing, speech) and human factors: perception, attention, memory, learning, decision-making, mental models, cognitive load, limitations and errors.
  2. The computer system. The hardware and software, including the user interface with its two components: input (how the user communicates needs) and output (how the computer conveys results).
  3. The interaction. The dialogue between user and system: user goals, actions, system responses, feedback, navigation and error handling (Interaction Design).
  4. The task and context of use. What the user wants to do, and the environment, device, age and experience of the users.
  5. Design and evaluation. Design principles, prototyping and evaluation, which keep the system usable and improve it.

Goal tying them together: improve usability and user experience.

What are multimodal interfaces, and why are they important?

Meaning. Multimodal interaction occurs when a system supports more than one method of interaction. Examples: voice + touch, voice + gesture, touch + vision, speech + facial expression.

Example from the notes. A user says "Open my results." The system displays the results, and the user then touches the screen to select a particular semester.

Why they are important:

  • More flexible. The user picks the method that suits the moment (voice when hands are busy, touch when it is noisy).
  • More natural. People communicate with several senses at once in daily life, so the interface feels closer to that.
  • More inclusive. A user who cannot use one method can use another (supports users with disabilities).
  • Reflects modern interfaces. Modern UIs from 2015 onward combine voice, touch and vision (Siri, Alexa, AR/VR, chatbots).

What is a heuristic evaluation?

Definition. A heuristic evaluation is an analytical (expert-based) evaluation method in which experts examine an interface using established usability heuristics, such as Nielsen's 10 principles.

How it works (AAUA case study): 3 HCI experts reviewed the portal against Nielsen's 10 heuristics. They recorded the number of heuristic violations and a severity rating for each.

Findings in the case study:

  • Visibility of system status: no confirmation or loading feedback.
  • Match with the real world: "Programme fees" confused users.
  • Consistency: buttons and icons varied in shape and function.
  • Recognition over recall: too many hidden functions.
  • Help and documentation: no guidance.

Why it is used: experts can quickly spot violations of usability standards without needing large user groups. It is often combined with usability testing, since the notes advise combining methods.

Different kinds of people interact with things in different ways. Itemize and explain factors that determine how people interact with things.

These are the human factors and cognitive aspects of HCI. Human factors are the physical, psychological and cognitive characteristics, capabilities and limitations of people that influence how they interact with computer systems.

FactorExplanationDesign implication
1. PerceptionReceiving and interpreting information through sight, hearing and touch.Readable text, good contrast, meaningful icons; do not rely on sound alone; big, well-spaced touch targets.
2. AttentionAbility to concentrate on some information and ignore the rest. It is limited.Highlight important items, group related items, avoid clutter and distractions.
3. MemoryStoring, retaining and retrieving information. Recognition is easier than recall.Show visible options (a Save button) instead of requiring Ctrl+S from memory.
4. LearningHow users acquire the knowledge and skills to use a system.Make basic tasks obvious; "Question 5 of 50" shows position.
5. Decision-makingUsers constantly decide what to select, continue or cancel.Clear labels ("Proceed to Payment" not "Proceed"), clear consequences, warnings.
6. Mental modelsThe user's internal understanding of how a system works, from experience.Use familiar metaphors (trash bin = delete).
7. Cognitive loadMental effort needed to understand information or perform a task.Break tasks into steps, use defaults, show options, give feedback.
8. Human limitationsUsers forget, get distracted, misread, mistype, tire, and decide under pressure.Do not assume perfect behaviour.
9. Human errorsActions that produce unintended results.Prevent, detect, explain, and help users recover.

Other differences between people (from the notes): age, skill level, background, culture, abilities and disabilities, level of experience, and the context of use (device and environment). This is why designers ask "Who are the users? What devices will they use? What level of experience do they have?"

What is a Graphical User Interface? Explain its purpose and the types of visual metaphors used.

GUI. A type of user interface that lets users interact with digital devices using graphical elements such as windows, icons, buttons and menus, rather than relying only on text commands. It is the foundation of modern interface design.

Purpose: make interaction more intuitive, visual and accessible; reduce the need to memorise commands; present functions using visual metaphors.

Visual metaphor: a design technique where real-world objects or concepts are shown visually to help users understand how to interact (trash bin, folders, desktop, printer icon, scissors icon, battery bar).

TypeMeaningExample
Object-basedReal physical objectsFolder, trash bin, desktop
Action-basedBehaviours or operationsDrag-and-drop mimics moving files

Why are design principles needed in interface design?

  1. Guide consistency. Buttons, menus and icons look and act the same across screens.
  2. Reduce user errors. Disable unavailable options, show clear error messages, confirm destructive actions.
  3. Improve learnability and efficiency. Clear instructions, simple recognisable icons, logical grouping.
  4. Provide a framework for evaluation. They act as checkpoints in prototyping, usability testing and interface critique.
  5. Support user-centred design. They keep focus on user needs, accessibility and context of use (age, device, environment), making systems more inclusive.

Definition: design principles are guidelines or best practices that help designers build systems that are usable (easy to learn and use) and consistent (uniform and predictable).

Design evaluation

★★★ Very likely10 marks

What is design evaluation in HCI and why is it carried out?

Design evaluation is the systematic assessment of an interface or interactive system to determine how well it supports users in achieving their tasks, whether it meets usability standards and user expectations, and which areas need improvement before or after deployment.

Why evaluate:

  • Identify usability problems early.
  • Measure user satisfaction.
  • Ensure accessibility and effectiveness.
  • Validate design decisions with evidence.
  • Save cost and time by reducing future rework.
  • Verify users can complete tasks, and find where they struggle or make errors.
  • Improve the overall user experience.

  • Focus on user goals and context.

Human Factors

Human factors and cognition

Part 2: perception, attention, memory, learning, decisions, mental models, load and errors. Q6b territory.

★★★ Very likely5 to 10 marks

What are human factors, and why does HCI consider them?

Human factors are the physical, psychological and cognitive characteristics, capabilities and limitations of people that influence how they interact with computer systems and other technologies.

Why: a system can be technically correct and still be hard to use if it ignores the abilities and limits of its users. Good design requires understanding how people think, perceive, remember, learn, decide and respond to errors.

They help designers answer: How does a user perceive information on screen? How much can they remember? How do they know what to click? How do they learn a new system? Why do they make errors? How can the system prevent them?

Purpose: design systems that fit human capabilities instead of forcing users to adapt to poorly designed systems.

★★ Likely10 marks

Explain attention and cognitive load in HCI and how designers can support them.

Attention is the ability to concentrate on particular information while ignoring other information. It is limited, so an interface with too many buttons, images, colours, animations, notifications and advertisements makes it hard to find the important item. Example: a portal with 25 equally prominent buttons makes a student hunt for Course Registration.

Support attention by: highlighting important information, grouping related elements, reducing unnecessary information, appropriate spacing, visual hierarchy, avoiding unnecessary animation.

Cognitive load is the mental effort needed to understand information or perform a task. A course registration system that needs students to remember course codes, cross unrelated pages, re-enter information, remember selections and offers no progress indicator adds unnecessary load.

Reduce cognitive load by: breaking complex tasks into steps, using familiar terms, meaningful defaults, showing options, grouping information, clear navigation, feedback, fewer choices.

★★ Likely5 to 10 marks

What is a mental model? How do learning and decision-making influence interface design?

Mental model: the user's internal understanding or expectation of how a system works, built from previous experience, real-world objects, familiar software, social conventions and other digital systems. Example: a trash-bin icon means delete because users know the physical bin. It relates to visual metaphors (folders, trash bins, desktops).

Learning: a system should let a new user perform basic tasks without excessive training. Example: showing "Question 5 of 50" in an online exam.

Decision-making: users decide constantly (select, continue, cancel, which payment method, delete the file?). Good design presents relevant information, uses clear labels, avoids unnecessary choices, groups alternatives, gives warnings and makes consequences clear. "Proceed to Payment" is better than "Proceed".

Discuss human limitations and human errors. How should a good HCI system deal with errors?

Limitations. Users may forget information, become distracted, misread, mistype, click the wrong button, misinterpret an icon, become tired, or decide under pressure. This does not mean users are incapable. It means systems should not assume perfect human behaviour.

Human error: an action that produces an unintended result.

A good HCI system should:

  1. Prevent errors (constraints, confirmation).
  2. Detect errors.
  3. Explain errors.
  4. Help users recover from errors.

Interaction Design

Interaction design

Part 2: IxD concepts including the affordance/signifier pair examiners love.

★★★ Very likely10 marks

Define Interaction Design (IxD). What does it consider?

Interaction Design is the process of designing the behaviour, structure and responses of interactive systems so that users can accomplish their goals effectively and satisfactorily. It focuses on how users and systems behave during interaction.

It considers: user goals, user needs, user actions, system responses, feedback, navigation, errors and context of use.

User-centred thinking. Designers begin by understanding the people who will use the system. They ask: Who are the users? What do they want to achieve? What problems do they experience? What environment and devices will they use? What level of experience do they have? This prevents designing from the developer's own assumptions.

The process is iterative, not strictly linear.

Prototyping

Prototyping

Part 2: fidelity, wireframes vs mock-ups, and why prototype at all.

★★★ Very likely10 marks

What is prototyping in HCI? Why is it important and when should prototypes be used?

Prototyping is the process of creating an early representation of a proposed system or interface to explore, communicate and evaluate design ideas. It ranges from a simple paper drawing to a highly interactive digital representation. Designers do not need to build the complete system before testing an idea.

Importance:

  1. Test ideas early.
  2. Obtain user feedback.
  3. Identify usability problems.
  4. Compare alternative designs.
  5. Test navigation.
  6. Improve interaction.
  7. Reduce development risks.
  8. Reduce the cost of correcting design problems.

When to use: early and throughout the design process, to test navigation, layout, user flows, interaction, labels, buttons, forms and information structure.
